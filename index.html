Wookhyun Kwon권욱현

I work across instrumental and electroacoustic composition. The work stays with what remains as an expectation between an instruction, the sensation of the body carrying it out, and the sound perceived in performance. Instruments, physical effort, and the performance setting become a single material. The expectation takes form through them.

I was born in Uijeongbu, Korea, and work between Chicago and Seoul. The work has been presented in New York, Berlin, Darmstadt, Seoul, and elsewhere. I'm pursuing a PhD in Composition and Music Technology at Northwestern University, studying with Jay Alan Yim, Alex Mincek, and Stephan Moore, and hold degrees from Manhattan School of Music and Kookmin University.

statement...

A score establishes what is expected. It gives precise form to something that cannot be realized exactly. Performance brings that expectation into real time. A concert is controlled to realize the expectation, but never fully controllable. As the work unfolds, instruments, bodies, and relationships shape how its instructions are carried out. Effort, timing, response, and control shape what happens.

Expectation forms my work. It also shapes how the materials function. These expectations begin with familiar assumptions. A pattern will continue. An instruction can be carried out. The same signal will produce the same response. Some are set by musical convention: a performer responds to a cue, an instrument sounds as expected, and an ensemble stays together. The work uses these conventions as material, against what they are for. It keeps their forms but empties their functions.

My work isolates and magnifies different parts of this process. Each score draws one of these expectations into a specific task. In one, a physical movement is repeated in one direction. The restriction intensifies the expectation of its return or repetition. In another, an instrument follows the shape of a sound it cannot physically reproduce. That failure is built into the task. Elsewhere, a conductor's cue stays the same while the ensemble's response changes. A familiar convention remains in place after its function has changed: the gesture stays recognizable while control moves somewhere else.

In sound, an expectation takes form over time. The difference between expectation and execution becomes audible through physical effort in performance. Neither exists without the other, so both stay in the piece. The instruction, the physical action that carries it out, and the resulting sound remain connected, even as the relation among them changes. The work is built from these relations.
